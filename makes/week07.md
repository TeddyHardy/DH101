## The Map

**I chose to map:** Data Centers, Mining Sites, and Labor Sites

![Google My Maps map of AI data centers, mining sites and labor sites]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/week07/image1.png)

**Data Centers:** Most of the data centers are in the United States, but there are also various locations across Europe, and Southeast Asia. Data Centers are built in the United States because that is where the AI companies are. Amazon, Microsoft, Google, OpenAI are all American, so these companies chose to build close to home. There is lots of cheap land in the United States. Iowa, Texas, Oregon, and Virginia have open land and cheap electricity, and some of it is renewable. They are also being built in Europe and Southeast Asia because data needs to travel physically, so a user in Singapore gets faster service from a nearby server rather than one in the United States. Cold places like Iceland and Ireland countries cool servers for free and have renewable power.

**Mining Sites:** Most of the mining sites I was looking at were in South America. This is due to geology, prices, and battery demand. The “Lithium Triangle” is in Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia where there are huge salt flats full of lithium brine. It is very cheap to extract as companies can pump the brine into ponds and let the sun evaporate the water. This is much cheaper than hard-rock mining, but one downside is it uses a lot of water. There are also mining sites being built in Africa and Asia. This is also because of the geology, as the DRC produces around 70% of the world cobalt, a key material in batteries. In Asia, China has large deposits and has spent decades building up mining and processing. They control most of the world’s rare earth mining and almost all the processing. These metals are going into the chips and electronics that AI runs on.

**Labor Sites:** Labor sites are in Asia and Africa due to lower wages. A company in San Francisco can pay a worker in Africa or Asia a small fraction of a US wage. Kenya, Philippines, and India all have large English-speaking population, and most AI training data and social media content is in English.

## Research Documentation

I used Claude AI to help determine which locations I would put on my map. My prompt was “Chose 10 of the biggest Data Centers, 6 of the biggest mining sites, and 4 labor sites and give the locations to put into my google map.” At first, it struggled to find locations that were related to this topic, but I got rid of that issue by using reports from human rights groups, The Washington Post, and Global Witness to help locate some sites. The hardest locations to find were the labor sites. This was due to the fact that labor is a controversial subject as many workers at these sites are treated unfairly and underpaid. Companies always release information regarding how big and powerful they are, but hide controversial topics like who is doing the physical work for them. Almost everything I found about these workers came from journalists, not the big companies themselves. For example, Time.com stated that OpenAI used Kenyan workers on less that $2 per hour to make ChatGPT less toxic.

What surprised me the most was that the “cloud” can is very physical and uses a good number of resources. Chat GPT’s model was trained using water from Iowa, and Googles servers used around a third of one Oregon city’s water. I also believe that it is unfair that the harm falls on communities with the least power. Many mines are located near indigenous communities, and these people now live in dangerous and harmful living situations that were not there before these mines were created. Another thing that surprised me was that people in these communities are fighting back. A community in the Netherlands stopped Meta from building a data center, and Indigenous groups located in Chile went to court with a lithium company over water scarcity.

## Artist Statement

> My map argues that AI infrastructure is not “virtual.” It is built on land, uses water and minerals, and relies on human labor. By following AI from the lithium and cobalt mines, to data centers, the map reveals a chain that most people never see.
> 
> If I had mapped the corporate headquarters but not the mines and labor sites, the story and background would seem simple. That is the version that these tech companies prefer. Mapping mines, data centers, and labor shifts attention from the major companies who design AI to the people and places that absorb its costs.
> 
> This connects to Data Feminism’s argument that data is never neutral and always reflects power. The hardest locations to research were water and labor sites, showing that the powerful companies decide what is publicized. My map does the opposite; it makes the official data visible. I used different colors for each layer so that the readers can see how these sites connect across the world. Every mining and labor pin fall in areas where poverty is already an issue, while profits flow to the powerful companies in the United States. My map still has blind spots; A pin cannot show what a worker in the south goes through on a daily basis, and there are many sites that stay hidden because the major companies want to keep them hidden.
